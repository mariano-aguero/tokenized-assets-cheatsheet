Tokenized Assets Cheatsheet

A tokenized asset is not a coin with a story attached. It is a claim on something off the chain, wrapped in a legal structure, recorded in a register, and represented by a token that carries the rules of who may hold it.

This page follows one holding from issuance to exit: who is allowed in, what the token actually enforces, where the truth is kept, how the money leg settles, and what happens on the day the chain and the paperwork disagree.

Every block below hangs off one of these stages. The order matters: most of the hard parts in tokenization are decided before a single token is minted.

01The register is the asset, the token is a representation

This is the single idea that reorders everything else. Get it wrong and you will build a system that is technically correct and legally meaningless.

Ownership of a security is whatever the official register says it is. In traditional markets a transfer agent keeps that register. Tokenization does not remove the register, it moves it, and the interesting question for any project is always the same one: is the chain the register, or a mirror of it?

The chain as the register

The token balance is the official holding. The transfer agent operates on chain, and a peer to peer transfer between two verified holders updates the register with no intervention. This is what the regulated end of the market is moving toward, and it is what makes 24/7 markets possible.

Costs you: every legal requirement now has to be expressible as code, and mistakes are on a public ledger.

The chain as a mirror

The register stays in the issuer's books and the token is a claim ticket that tracks it. Simpler to launch, and most early projects are here whether they admit it or not.

Costs you: two sources of truth that can drift, and a reconciliation problem that looks exactly like the one in payments.

The test: if the token contract and the issuer's records disagree tomorrow morning, which one does a court believe? Whatever you answer is your real architecture, and everything else is implementation detail.

02Compliance lives inside the token

In a bearer token, a transfer is a balance update. In a security token, a transfer is a request that the contract is allowed to refuse, and that refusal is the product.

standardwhere the rules liveshape
ERC-20nowhereBearer. Anyone can hold, anyone can send. Useless as a security wrapper on its own.
ERC-1404a single check functionThe minimal restricted token: a hook that returns a reason code. Easy to ship, hard to grow into.
ERC-1400mostly off chainPartitions or tranches, documents, and an off-chain compliance service the contract consults. Flexible, and the flexibility is where implementations diverge.
ERC-3643 (T-REX)fully on chainAn identity registry plus pluggable compliance modules. A transfer that breaks a rule is rejected by the contract before it executes. Finalized ERC, with an ISO standardization effort underway.
ERC-7518on chain, partitionedPartitioned multi-asset tokens with dynamic vouchers, aimed at issuers that need many classes inside one contract.
ERC-7943 (uRWA)a minimal hook setThe newest of these and now Final. Rather than a framework, it defines the few functions everyone was writing anyway: can this account transact, is this transfer allowed, freeze, forced transfer. Extends ERC-20, ERC-721 or ERC-1155, and its naming is backward compatible with ERC-3643 and ERC-7518.
Token-2022on chain, SolanaTransfer hooks, accounts frozen by default, and a permanent delegate for clawback. The same idea in a different ecosystem.
The thing to notice is that all of them answer one question: where does the rule get evaluated, and who can see it? Fully on chain is auditable by anyone and costs gas on every transfer. Off chain is cheap and private, and asks you to trust an oracle of eligibility that nobody outside can inspect. The gas difference is not theoretical: a minimal hook runs in the single-digit thousands, while a modular registry check can cost an order of magnitude more, on every transfer, forever.

The ERC-1400 family, in pieces

ERC-1400 is usually cited as one thing and is really an umbrella over four, which is why two implementations that both claim it can be very different:

  • ERC-1594: the core security token, with the transfer that can be refused and a reason returned.
  • ERC-1410: partially fungible balances, so one holding can be split into tranches with different restrictions.
  • ERC-1643: document management, attaching and hashing the legal documents that the token depends on.
  • ERC-1644: the controller operation, which is the forced transfer power under another name.

If somebody says their token is ERC-1400, the useful question is which of the four they actually implemented.

Standards for the shape of the asset, not its rules

The ones above govern who may hold. These govern what the holding is, and they compose with the compliance layer rather than replacing it:

  • ERC-721 for a unique asset: one deed, one artwork, one loan.
  • ERC-1155 for many classes in one contract, useful when an issuer has dozens of series.
  • ERC-3525 semi-fungible: units that share a slot but carry their own value, which fits bonds and instruments with a face value per unit.
  • ERC-4626 the tokenized vault: a deposit and share accounting standard. It is not a security standard, but it is how most yield-bearing wrappers around a tokenized asset are built, and integrations expect it.
  • ERC-884 an older one worth knowing for context: tokens representing shares under Delaware corporate law, an early attempt at binding a token to a legal register.

Outside the EVM

Several chains put the controls at the protocol layer instead of in a contract, which changes the failure modes: there is less code to audit and less room to express an unusual rule.

chainwhat it gives you natively

Moving across chains

A restricted token that exists on two chains has to keep one register, and bridging by lock and mint quietly creates a second one. The standards that address this are transport rather than compliance: Chainlink CCIP with its cross-chain token model, LayerZero OFT and Wormhole NTT. None of them decides who may hold the asset, so the compliance layer still has to be evaluated on the destination chain.

Naming the thing: identifiers

The least discussed and most immediately practical part. A tokenized security needs to be identifiable by systems that have never heard of your contract address.

  • ISO 24165, the Digital Token Identifier: an ISO standard code for a specific token. ESMA mandated its use under MiCA in July 2024, which turned it from an idea into a compliance requirement in Europe.
  • ISIN for the instrument and LEI for the issuing entity: the tokenized version still needs both to be reportable anywhere in traditional finance.
  • CAIP-2 and CAIP-19: chain-agnostic identifiers for the network and the asset, which is what tooling uses when it has to name a token across ecosystems.

Open ended or closed: when does the token get created?

The most common confusion in this whole page. The asset already exists, so why is anything being minted? Because there are two supply models and they behave differently.

modelwhen tokens are createdwhat it buys you
closedOnce. A fixed block is deposited, a fixed supply is minted, and from then on every trade is a transfer of existing units.Simple, and honest for an asset that is genuinely fixed: one building, one loan, one fund that is closed to new money. The price can drift from the underlying and nothing pulls it back.
open endedContinuously. A subscription buys the underlying and mints against it; a redemption burns and sells.The supply tracks demand, and that mint and burn is the arbitrage that keeps the token near the value of the thing it represents. Almost every tokenized treasury, fund and equity wrapper works this way.
What is actually prior: tokenization is the structure, not the minting. The legal wrapper, the custody agreement, the token contract and the compliance rules are all in place before anyone buys. What happens per trade is issuance or redemption inside that structure, and on the secondary market not even that: just a transfer.

03Identity, not addresses

A wallet is not an investor. Eligibility belongs to a person or an entity, and that person can hold several wallets, lose one, and still be the same holder.

The identity object

An on-chain identity contract holds claims signed by issuers you trust: this person passed KYC, is accredited, is resident here, is not on a list. The token asks the identity, not the address.

Fails as: whitelisting raw addresses, which breaks the first time somebody changes wallet.

Claims and their issuers

A claim is only as good as who signed it and when. Claims expire, jurisdictions change, and an accreditation from two years ago is not evidence today. Expiry is a design decision, not an afterthought.

Fails as: claims with no expiry, so the register slowly fills with people who no longer qualify.

Compliance modules

Rules composed as small units: maximum holders per jurisdiction, minimum holding, lock-up windows, country allow and deny lists, daily volume caps. Each is independently testable, which is the only way this stays sane.

Fails as: one monolithic check that nobody can change without redeploying the token.

The holder count problem

Several exemptions cap how many holders of a given type an issuer may have. That cap has to be enforced at transfer time, on chain, forever, and it is the rule people most often discover late.

Fails as: a successful secondary market that quietly breaks the exemption the whole structure depends on.

04The powers the issuer keeps

Here is where a security token stops looking like crypto. A regulated asset must be recoverable, and recoverable means somebody can move your tokens without your key.

powerwhat it doeswhy it exists
freeze addressStops a specific holder from sending or receiving.Court order, sanctions hit, suspected fraud, an account under investigation.
freeze partialLocks part of a balance and leaves the rest usable.Lock-ups, pledged collateral, a disputed portion.
forced transferMoves tokens without the holder's signature. The destination still has to be a verified identity, which is the one guardrail the override keeps.Inheritance, court-ordered transfer, correcting an error the issuer is liable for.
recoveryMoves the balance, the frozen amount and the identity link from a lost wallet to a new one bound to the same identity.Someone lost a key. In a bearer token the money is gone. In a security, the holding survives, because the register says who owns it.
pauseStops all transfers of the token.An incident, a halted market, a discovered bug.
The trade, stated plainly: you get recoverability and you give up censorship resistance. That is not a bug in the design, it is the design. A security has an issuer, an issuer has obligations, and obligations require somebody able to act. If your users expect bearer-token behaviour, the surprise happens at the worst moment.

05The money leg

Every transfer of value has two legs: the asset and the cash. Most of the risk in traditional settlement comes from those two legs happening at different times.

Delivery versus payment, natively

When both legs are tokens on the same chain, one transaction can move the asset and the cash together. Either both happen or neither does, and counterparty risk between the legs disappears rather than being managed.

Fails as: two transactions with a hopeful gap in between, which is the old problem with new tooling.

T+0 is not the same as atomic

Same day settlement means both legs land today. Atomic means they land in the same instant, indivisibly. The first is an operational improvement, the second removes a class of risk.

Fails as: selling T+0 while the cash leg still clears through a bank the next morning.

When the cash leg is off chain

If payment arrives by wire and the asset moves on chain, you are back to a two-phase problem with compensations, a reconciliation, and an exception queue. The tokenized side does not save you.

Fails as: minting on a payment notification that later fails or is recalled.

Weekend liquidity

A 24/7 asset settled against a five-day cash rail has a gap every weekend. Stablecoins exist in this stack mostly to close that gap, which is also why so much tokenized treasury volume settles in them.

Fails as: promising instant redemption and discovering the banking calendar on a Saturday.

06Servicing: the part that never stops

Issuance is a project. Servicing is a job, every day, for the life of the asset, and it is where most operational cost actually lives.

  • Price and NAV: a fund token needs a value, published on a schedule, usually delivered on chain by an oracle. A stale NAV is not a cosmetic bug: it is a mispriced subscription or redemption.
  • Subscriptions and redemptions: minting and burning against the register at each window, with the cash leg on the other side.
  • Yield: paid three ways, and they are not interchangeable. A rebasing balance that grows, a distribution of a separate token, or an accruing price where the token count stays fixed and the value rises. Integrations break on the first, tax treatment differs on all three.
  • Corporate actions: splits, conversions, redemptions at maturity, restructurings. The traditional world has centuries of edge cases here and the chain has none of them built in.
  • Reporting: statements, tax documents, holder lists. Boring, mandatory, and a real integration surface.

07Primary, secondary, and the liquidity that usually is not there

The pitch for tokenization is usually liquidity. The reality is that liquidity is a market structure problem, and a token does not create one.

  • Primary issuance happens under an exemption, and the exemption sets the rules the token has to enforce: who may buy, how many holders, how long they must hold, which jurisdictions are excluded.
  • Lock-ups are the most common enforced rule, and the first thing a compliance module implements.
  • Secondary trading of a regulated token needs a venue licensed for it. In the US that is an alternative trading system, and a firm that wants to do all of it holds several licenses at once: transfer agent for the register, broker-dealer to intermediate, ATS to run the venue.
  • The hard truth: most tokenized assets trade thinly or not at all. The instruments that took off are the ones where the asset is already liquid and the token adds settlement speed, not the ones where the token was supposed to conjure a market out of an illiquid asset.

09How two companies actually do it

Two different answers to the same question, and the contrast explains the space better than any definition.

Securitize: own the whole regulated stack

Securitize is vertically integrated in the boring sense that matters: it holds SEC registrations across a transfer agent, a broker-dealer, an alternative trading system, an investment adviser and fund administration. That combination is the product. The transfer agent registration is what lets the on-chain record be the register rather than a mirror of one, and the ATS is what lets the resulting token trade at all.

On the technical side their DS Protocol embeds compliance in the token itself: issuance, transfers restricted to verified holders, dividends, voting and secondary trading. Their flagship is BlackRock's BUIDL, a tokenized money market fund that became the largest tokenized real world asset, with the firm reporting over $3B in tokenized assets on the platform.

What to take from it: in tokenization the licenses are the architecture. A team that cannot name which registered entity holds the register is describing a mirror, whatever the deck says.

Rain: tokenize your own balance sheet to fix settlement

Rain runs stablecoin-linked card programs: an API that lets fintechs issue cards, hold digital dollar accounts and move money across borders. The tokenization part is not a product they sell, it is how they solved their own problem. They tokenized their credit card receivables and moved settlement with Visa to USDC, which let them settle seven days a week instead of on banking days.

That is the least glamorous and most convincing use of tokenization in this page: not a new asset class, but an existing balance sheet item turned into something that can move on a weekend.

What to take from it: the winning cases so far are the ones where tokenization removes a calendar or a settlement window, not the ones where it promises liquidity for something illiquid.

10What breaks

Stale or wrong NAV

Subscriptions and redemptions priced off an oracle that has not updated, or has updated with a bad value. Every mint and burn at that price is a real loss to somebody, and it is not reversible on chain.

The legal wrapper disagrees with the chain

A transfer that the contract allowed but the jurisdiction did not, or a holder who stopped qualifying and nothing on chain noticed. The chain is consistent and wrong at the same time.

One leg lands

Whenever the two legs are not atomic, some fraction of trades ends with the asset moved and the cash missing, or the reverse. That fraction is your exception queue, and it needs an owner.

Redemption gates

The underlying asset is not as liquid as the token pretends. Redemption windows, gates and notice periods exist in the fund documents and often nowhere in the interface.

Composability that should not compose

A restricted token deposited into an open protocol: the pool contract is not a verified holder, the transfer either reverts or, worse, succeeds and breaks the exemption. Most integration pain lives here.

Recovery abused

The same power that saves a holder who lost a key can move tokens that were not lost. Who may call it, under what evidence, with what record, is a governance question you answer before launch or explain afterwards.

12Build order

  1. Settle the legal structure first, including which entity holds the register and under which exemption the thing is sold. Everything below is downstream of that answer.
  2. Identity before token. The registry and the claim issuers exist before a single token is minted, because the first mint is already a restricted transfer.
  3. One compliance module, tested alone. Usually the jurisdiction allow list or the lock-up. Prove the reject path in a test before adding the second.
  4. Mint to a verified holder and confirm the register and the chain agree.
  5. The transfer that must fail. Write the test where a valid holder sends to an unverified address and the contract refuses. That test is the product.
  6. The money leg, atomic if both legs are on chain, with a reconciliation and an exception queue if they are not.
  7. The repair powers, with the policy written down: who calls freeze, who approves a forced transfer, what evidence a recovery requires.
  8. Servicing, which is where the asset spends its life: NAV, yield, statements.
